## Hacker News

**Title:** Headroom: what is left when servers drop out

**URL:** https://cameleer.ing/headroom/

**First comment (by the owner):**

> Author here. Yesterday's Microsoft 365 outage was, in Microsoft's words, not more traffic but
> servers going unavailable and straining the rest, and restarted servers fell over again once
> traffic reached them. We don't know the root cause yet; the page says so and will be updated when
> the review is out.
>
> The general point: how much a fleet survives losing a third of itself depends on what each
> request costs and how fast a node comes back. That can be measured before an incident. The page
> has three tests (degraded fleet, restart under load, connection storm) and our own numbers,
> including where we lose: our HTTP engine handles overload badly, 10th of 19 on answers within
> 1 ms.
>
> The broader project is Cameleering: making widely used software carry the same load on less and
> publishing every result against the original, losses included (https://cameleer.ing/ledger/).
> Everything is Apache-2.0.

## LinkedIn

> Yesterday Microsoft 365 went down for hours. Microsoft said it wasn't extra traffic. It was the
> same traffic on fewer servers, and servers that came back fell over again when the load returned.
>
> That's a headroom problem, and headroom is set by what each request costs. Halve the CPU and
> memory a request needs and you have doubled your margin without buying a server.
>
> We don't know the root cause yet, and this isn't a guess at it. But every team can measure its
> own margin before its own bad day: take a third of the servers away under production load,
> restart one under load, reconnect every client at once. Write down what happens.
>
> I wrote up the arithmetic, the tests, and our own numbers, including where we're still behind:
> https://cameleer.ing/headroom/
>
> This is what Cameleering is about: make software need less, then prove it against the original.
> https://cameleer.ing/
